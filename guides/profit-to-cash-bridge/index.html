Trace profit to cash movement before drawing a conclusion

An owner can see a profit on the income statement and still wonder why the bank balance did not increase by the same amount. That question deserves a clear explanation rather than a quick reassurance that profit and cash are different. The useful next step is to connect a defined reporting period, the relevant statements, and the transactions that explain the movement. A small worked example can make the conversation easier to prepare.

This guide uses a deliberately simplified fictional business. It is an educational bridge, not a complete statement of cash flows, a tax calculation, a valuation, or a recommendation about distributions or borrowing. The numbers do not describe Mark Simonsen's clients or expected business performance. Use the example to understand questions you can take to the person responsible for your actual financial statements, with the appropriate records and accounting context.

The SEC's introductory financial-statement guide distinguishes statements about a period's earnings, a point-in-time financial position, and cash movement. OpenStax's finance text discusses the operating, investing, and financing sections of a cash-flow statement. Those sources provide background. The specific numbers, assumptions, comparison cases, and review worksheet below are original teaching examples rather than a reproduction of a reported company statement.

Define the period and the cash you are comparing

Choose a beginning date and an ending date. Use the same period when asking about the profit figure and the change in cash. A year-to-date profit compared with one month's bank movement will not answer a coherent question. Record the entity, reporting dates, report basis, and filters so an adviser can identify the actual information being discussed.

Define the cash population too. One bank account is not necessarily every cash account in the business. Transfers between accounts can look like money arriving or leaving when only one account is viewed. Ask the responsible professional which balances should be compared for the particular question. In the fictional exercise here, the cash total is explicitly defined and there are no transfers between included and omitted accounts.

Do not assume a screenshot establishes a reconciled or final balance. Bring the relevant reports and identify whether the period is closed, still being reviewed, or subject to adjustment. A bridge built from preliminary figures may be useful as a draft, but it should remain labeled accordingly. The first practical improvement is often making the comparison consistent before explaining any difference.

State the fictional assumptions before calculating

Our sample business begins the period with cash of 20,000 and ends with cash of 23,000. Its income statement reports net profit of 10,000. For this exercise, the only noncash expense adjustment is depreciation of 1,000. Accounts receivable increase by 4,000, accounts payable increase by 2,000, equipment is purchased for 5,000 cash, and loan principal of 1,000 is paid.

The example assumes no other cash flows or reconciling items. There are no owner contributions, distributions, new loan proceeds, inventory changes, prepaid changes, additional accrual changes, taxes, interest, disposals, or other adjustments in this invented dataset. That is a teaching boundary, not a description of what a normal business must contain. Actual statements often require substantially more information and professional judgment.

Write these assumptions beside the example rather than hiding them in a footnote. Without them, a reader could mistake a short bridge for a universally complete formula. The purpose is to follow each selected movement and check the arithmetic. The fact that this fictional example reconciles does not establish that copying its categories will reconcile a real set of books.

Begin with the reported profit

Start the bridge at the fictional net profit of 10,000. Keep the label intact. Do not rename it cash generated, available distribution, or owner take-home pay. Those are different questions. The starting profit comes from the selected income statement under the exercise's assumptions, and the remaining lines explain why the period's cash movement differs.

A useful review note identifies where the profit figure came from and whether it matches the version the adviser is using. If one report was regenerated after adjustments and another was not, the mismatch can create an apparent cash-flow mystery. Preserve the report date and version. A careful source reference is more useful than manually changing the starting number to make the bridge agree.

In a real discussion, ask what the reported earnings measure includes. A management subtotal, net income, EBITDA, seller-adjusted earnings, and a tax-return amount should not be substituted for one another without explanation. This guide uses only the stated fictional net-profit amount. If your question begins with a different measure, ask the professional to define the appropriate bridge rather than assuming the same starting line applies.

Add the selected noncash expense adjustment

The fictional profit includes depreciation expense of 1,000. In this example, that expense did not itself pay 1,000 out of cash during the period, so the bridge adds 1,000 back in moving from the selected profit toward cash from operations. The running amount becomes 11,000. This is an explanation of the exercise, not a determination of depreciation or tax treatment for a particular asset.

Do not confuse the adjustment with a claim that equipment costs nothing. The example separately includes the actual 5,000 equipment cash purchase later in the bridge. Adding the noncash expense adjustment and showing the cash purchase answer different parts of the question. Leaving out the purchase would produce an incomplete explanation even though the depreciation line was handled as the exercise specifies.

For an actual business, ask the adviser which noncash adjustments apply and where they are supported. Do not simply copy a depreciation total from an unrelated tax schedule into a management bridge. The relevant statements and basis may differ. Keep the source question explicit so the reviewer can identify the appropriate amount and any other adjustments the simplified example omits.

Explain the receivable movement

Accounts receivable increase by 4,000 in the fictional dataset. Under the exercise's accrual assumptions, that increase represents a difference between the selected earnings and cash collection timing. The bridge subtracts 4,000, taking the running amount from 11,000 to 7,000. This is not a conclusion that the receivables are uncollectible or that the business has a collection problem.

The practical question for a real review is what explains the movement. Ask for the relevant receivable support and whether the beginning and ending balances cover the same entity and scope. A change can contain details the short bridge does not show. The reviewer needs to understand those details before using the movement in a complete cash-flow explanation or drawing a conclusion about operations.

Keep the distinction between an arithmetic adjustment and a management judgment. A subtraction in the bridge explains one relationship in the selected dataset. It does not decide whether credit terms should change, whether a customer should be contacted, or whether a balance needs another accounting action. Those decisions require the actual records, circumstances, and responsible professional input.

Explain the payable movement

Accounts payable increase by 2,000 in our fictional example. Under the stated assumptions, the bridge adds that movement because the selected expenses and the related cash payments do not occur in the same amount during the period. The running amount rises from 7,000 to 9,000. This is the exercise's simplified cash-from-operations subtotal.

The addition is not free money. An outstanding obligation may require a later payment, and the bridge does not determine when that payment is due or whether the business has enough resources for it. An owner reviewing the result should ask about the actual payable schedule and other commitments through the appropriate financial process rather than treating the positive adjustment as a recommendation to spend more.

In a real review, confirm what the balance movement contains and whether other liabilities or noncash events affect the explanation. The fictional dataset excludes those complications so the mechanism is visible. A reviewer should not assume every change in a payable balance has the same meaning in every business. Keep the supporting reports and unresolved questions with the working bridge.

Separate the equipment cash purchase

The sample business pays 5,000 cash for equipment during the period. The exercise shows that payment after the 9,000 operating subtotal, as a selected investing cash outflow. Subtracting it leaves 4,000 before the final financing line. The treatment shown is part of the specified teaching example and should not be used as an instruction for classifying an actual purchase without review.

Notice why the earlier depreciation adjustment does not remove the need for this line. The income statement includes the selected expense, while the bridge also needs to explain the actual cash paid for the equipment. A reader who stops at the operating subtotal would miss an important reason that the ending cash did not increase by 9,000.

For your own records, bring the relevant purchase documentation and ask how it appears in the financial statements and cash-flow presentation. Do not infer tax deductibility, a depreciation election, or an investment return from the fact that cash was paid. Those are separate questions. The bridge should help identify where the cash went without pretending to resolve every accounting and tax issue associated with the asset.

Separate the loan principal payment

The fictional business also pays 1,000 of loan principal. The exercise shows this as a selected financing cash outflow after the equipment payment. Subtracting it from the remaining 4,000 produces a net cash increase of 3,000. The example assumes no interest and no new borrowing, so there are no additional loan-related lines to interpret in this small dataset.

An actual loan payment may contain different components. Bring the lender's support and ask the responsible professional to explain the amounts relevant to the statements. Do not treat the entire bank withdrawal as interchangeable with an income-statement expense or assume the example's no-interest condition applies to your business. The purpose of the line is to make one specified cash movement visible.

If a real bridge does not agree, avoid creating another line called loan adjustment merely to fill the gap. Identify the actual transaction, its support, and how it was recorded. A balancing entry without an explanation can make the report look complete while hiding the question the owner was trying to understand. Preserve the difference until the responsible person can resolve it appropriately.

Reconcile the beginning and ending cash

The complete fictional arithmetic is 10,000 plus 1,000 minus 4,000 plus 2,000 minus 5,000 minus 1,000, giving a net increase of 3,000. Beginning cash of 20,000 plus that increase equals ending cash of 23,000. Each line has a defined role in the example, and no unexplained balancing amount is used to force the result.

That equality is a necessary check for this dataset, but it is not proof that a real statement is complete or correctly classified. A duplicated amount and an omitted amount can offset. A cash population can be defined inconsistently. A source can be preliminary. Verification therefore needs both arithmetic and the source relationships agreed with the person responsible for the financial statements.

The downloadable fictional bridge shows the selected lines and running amounts so you can inspect the example without entering real business information. It is not an accounting-system import file or an approved financial statement. Keep your actual records in the appropriate protected environment and use the example to prepare a clearer question, not to substitute invented amounts for unresolved real ones.

Change one assumption and see what follows

First, change only the receivable increase from 4,000 to 6,000. Under all the other original assumptions, the operating subtotal becomes 7,000 and the net cash increase becomes 1,000. Ending cash would be 21,000. This is a mechanical sensitivity case, not a forecast that collections will change by that amount or a judgment about the quality of the receivables.

Second, return to the original receivable assumption and change only the equipment cash purchase from 5,000 to 8,000. The net cash increase becomes zero, so ending cash remains 20,000. The fictional profit still begins at 10,000 under the exercise's stated assumptions. The case illustrates why a cash outflow outside the selected profit measure can matter to the cash comparison.

Third, return to the original dataset and add fictional new loan proceeds of 5,000. The net cash increase becomes 8,000 and ending cash becomes 28,000. That extra cash is not additional profit in the example. The case does not recommend borrowing; it shows why a higher cash balance can arise from a financing source rather than from improved operating earnings.

Turn the bridge into questions for a CPA

Bring the reports, period, cash population, and source references rather than only a final number. Ask which movements explain the difference, which adjustments are missing from your draft, and which amounts need review. If the purpose is planning, distinguish the historical explanation from any proposed future scenario. A completed historical bridge does not automatically establish what cash will be available next month.

Ask what the remaining cash figure does not answer. It may not resolve restricted amounts, upcoming obligations, required reserves, taxes, or owner distributions. Those questions depend on the actual business and professional advice. Avoid describing the net change or ending balance as an automatically available owner payment. A clear bridge supports the discussion while preserving the boundary between explanation and a decision to act.

Confirm the actual engagement scope and responsible person directly. This guide does not claim that an individual professional has reviewed your figures or that a particular analysis is included in an engagement. The fictional example is a preparation tool, not a reported client result.

Frequently asked questions

Why can profit be positive while cash barely changes?

The selected earnings measure and cash movement can reflect different timing and different activities. The fictional example shows a noncash expense adjustment, receivable and payable changes, an equipment payment, and a principal payment. Actual businesses may need many other lines. Use consistent reports and ask the responsible professional to explain the movements rather than assuming one universal formula covers every case.

Is the operating subtotal the amount I can take out?

Not automatically. It is one subtotal in the example, before the selected investing and financing outflows and without a conclusion about obligations or owner distributions. Actual decisions need the relevant agreements, financial position, commitments, and professional advice. Do not turn a bridge subtotal into a promised paycheck or a recommendation about how much the owner should withdraw.

Does adding back depreciation mean the asset was free?

No. The example separately shows the actual equipment cash payment. The noncash adjustment and the payment explain different parts of the relationship between the stated profit and cash movement. For a real business, ask which amounts and presentation apply under the relevant accounting basis. The example does not decide depreciation, tax treatment, or the wisdom of the purchase.

Can I use one bank account balance as the ending cash total?

Only if that is the appropriately defined population for the question and is consistent with the reports being reviewed. A business may have other accounts or transfers that need consideration. Ask the responsible professional to confirm the scope and reconciliation status. A screenshot of one account should not silently become a complete financial-statement cash figure.

What should I do if my draft does not reconcile?

Preserve the difference and identify the reports, assumptions, and source movements used. Check period, entity, filters, and cash scope, then take the unresolved question to the responsible reviewer. Do not insert an unexplained balancing line or alter the books to match the draft. The gap is information about what still needs to be understood, not a request to make the arithmetic look tidy.

Is this a complete statement of cash flows?

No. It is a simplified educational bridge with explicit fictional assumptions and a limited set of movements. A complete statement and its classifications require the actual records and applicable accounting context. Use the example to understand the structure of a question and to prepare for review, while keeping the real professional analysis and any decision to act separate.

Prepare a source-to-question handoff

For the fictional profit line, the handoff points to the selected income statement and asks whether that is the earnings measure the reviewer intends to explain. For the receivable and payable lines, it points to the beginning and ending support and asks what is included in each movement. For the equipment and loan lines, it points to the purchase and lender records. The structure makes every number a question with a source rather than an isolated value in a spreadsheet.

Apply that structure to your own draft without assuming the same six lines will be sufficient. Write a short note beside any amount whose source or meaning is uncertain. The reviewer can then distinguish a missing document from a difference in classification or reporting scope. That is a more useful starting point than a file in which every unknown has been converted into a confident-looking number.

Keep one unchanged copy of the reports used to prepare the question. If the books are later adjusted, the adviser can explain whether the original question was answered by a correction, a better comparison, or a fuller cash-flow analysis. Name the versions clearly. A before-and-after comparison is only understandable when the team knows which reports were current at each stage.

Finally, ask the reviewer to summarize the explanation in language the owner can use accurately. A useful summary might identify the principal selected drivers and the limitations that remain, while avoiding a claim that all cash is available for a new commitment. The owner should be able to explain why the figures differ without turning the teaching bridge into a distribution policy or a forecast. That clarity is the practical outcome this preparation exercise is intended to support.
